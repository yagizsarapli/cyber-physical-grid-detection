\begin{abstract}
Distinguishing cyber false-data-injection attacks (FDIAs) from legitimate physical disturbances requires accurate detection, localization, and sufficiently fast end-to-end processing. We evaluate the marginal value of explicit topology-relational features across a synthetic 5-bus microgrid and standard IEEE 14-bus and 30-bus systems using deployable WLS residuals, validation-only model selection, and a matched topology-free residual-plus-prior ablation. Across 16 independent seeds, topology features are neutral on the 5-bus system (detection gap $-0.000\pm0.009$; localization $+0.003\pm0.017$) but produce small positive gaps at both IEEE systems (IEEE 14-bus: $+0.009\pm0.012$ detection, $+0.018\pm0.017$ localization; IEEE 30-bus: $+0.028\pm0.019$ detection, $+0.013\pm0.016$ localization). Four unadjusted 95\% confidence intervals exclude zero; three effects (IEEE-14 localization, IEEE-30 detection, IEEE-30 localization) remain significant under a Bonferroni correction, while IEEE-14 detection does not. Separately, pipeline profiling reduces median end-to-end latency from 42.98\,ms to 16.2--17.0\,ms, with p99 at 17.0--19.2\,ms but rare maxima of 89.1--92.7\,ms. Finally, neither feature set generalizes to a completely unseen attack type (0--1.3\% recall versus 91--100\% when represented in training). The results show that topology benefits are network-specific, upstream software costs dominate real-time latency, and unseen attack mechanisms remain a major limitation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modernizing power grids toward higher penetrations of inverter-based resources (PV, battery storage, grid-forming converters) changes the character of protection and monitoring in two ways relevant here. First, inverters provide far less fault current than synchronous generators, which strains protection schemes designed around synchronous-machine fault signatures \cite{liaqat2026protection}. Second, the same measurement infrastructure used for state awareness is now also an attack surface: an adversary who can spoof a subset of sensor readings can attempt a false-data-injection attack (FDIA) against the state estimator that is, by construction, statistically indistinguishable from a legitimate measurement under a residual-based bad-data test \cite{liu2011false}.

Recent incidents illustrate the operational relevance of cyber monitoring in renewable-heavy grids. In December 2025, a destructive cyberattack attributed to infrastructure overlapping with the Dragonfly/Static Tundra threat actor damaged controller firmware and deployed wiper malware across more than 30 Polish wind and solar farms and a combined heat-and-power plant serving nearly half a million customers; electricity production and heat supply were ultimately not disrupted \cite{certpolska2026incident}. Although that incident was an OT/firmware-integrity attack rather than a state-estimation FDIA, it illustrates the need to distinguish malicious anomalies from physical events under operational time constraints.

Operational monitoring therefore combines three questions: whether an anomaly is cyber or physical, where it originates, and whether a decision can be produced within a relevant computational budget. These questions are often treated separately. FDIA detection is commonly evaluated offline on fixed datasets; attack localization is studied less often; and end-to-end real-time feasibility has received comparatively limited attention. A recent benchmark \cite{abukhousa2026latency} found that deep classifiers for joint cyber-physical discrimination can be individually fast (sub-15\,ms) while the measured end-to-end pipeline remains much slower (50--90\,ms). In parallel, topology-aware methods ask whether explicit relational information between electrically connected measurements improves detection or localization beyond topology-free features.

We evaluate detection and localization across three networks and separately profile real-time feasibility on the 5-bus implementation. The evaluation enforces separation between relational and non-relational feature sets, uses deployable WLS residuals, and selects models exclusively on validation data. The latency study reproduces the qualitative finding of a fast classifier embedded in a slower pipeline \cite{abukhousa2026latency} and identifies a specific software bottleneck: rebuilding a fixed-structure measurement table every cycle. Replacing repeated object creation with in-place value updates accounts for most of the measured latency reduction.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item A 16-seed cross-network ablation showing small positive detection and localization gaps for explicit topology-relational features at both standard IEEE test systems (14-bus, 30-bus), but no corresponding benefit on either task at the small custom 5-bus microgrid (Sections~\ref{sec:results1} and \ref{sec:scale}), together with a quantitative redundancy analysis of the 5-bus result.
\item A root-cause profiling methodology for the ``fast model, slow pipeline'' gap identified in prior work, and a demonstrated fix that closes most of it without hardware acceleration (Section~\ref{sec:latency}).
\item A zero-day stress test showing that recall collapses when an entire attack family is withheld from training (Section~\ref{sec:zeroday}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Foundational FDIA and Stealth Constructions}
The threat model follows Liu, Ning \& Reiter's foundational FDIA construction \cite{liu2011false} and AC-consistent extensions \cite{anwar2016modeling}: an attacker with network-model knowledge constructs a measurement perturbation that preserves the WLS residual. Later work considers weaker attacker knowledge, including unknown line parameters \cite{du2021targeted} and topology-blind generative or physics-informed attacks \cite{zhao2025blind,falas2026learning}. Low-magnitude attacks near the sensor-noise floor are also studied explicitly \cite{li2026physically}; the hardened 5-bus setting in Section~\ref{sec:results1} targets this more difficult regime.

\subsection{Deep-Learning FDIA Detection}
CNN, LSTM, and transfer-learning architectures have been applied extensively to FDIA detection \cite{niu2018dynamic,li2021adversarial,xu2021detecting}, typically as binary classification on a fixed attack distribution. The \texttt{residual\_only} and \texttt{prior\_only} baselines used here are consistent with this setting. The present ablation isolates the marginal contribution of explicit topology-relational features beyond their topology-free combination, under deployable feature construction and validation-only model selection.

\subsection{Topology-Aware / Graph-Based State Estimation and Detection}
Topology-aware graph neural network (GNN) methods use graph convolution or attention to exploit network structure in state estimation \cite{moshtagh2025topology,moshtagh2022timesync,suri2025powergnn,yaniv2026robust}. Prior work presents a mixed picture. \cite{moshtagh2025topology} and an earlier, closely related study by an overlapping author group \cite{moshtagh2022timesync} compare their GNN against a same-feature ``regular'' DNN baseline (identical PMU-derived node features, no adjacency matrix), providing a correctly scoped topology ablation; \cite{suri2025powergnn}'s non-graph baseline, by contrast, does not clearly document an input-matched comparison. These studies show that topology ablation is not absent from the literature. The present analysis addresses a complementary question: whether explicitly relational features add information beyond a matched topology-free feature set before model architecture becomes a confounding factor. Section~\ref{sec:results1} also evaluates the linear recoverability of relational features from the topology-free representation as a model-independent redundancy diagnostic.

\subsection{Attack/Event Localization}
Localization work spans capsule networks for load-altering (not FDIA) attacks evaluated across 14/39/57-bus systems \cite{jahangir2022localizing}, moving-target-defense-assisted localization \cite{chen2022localization}, and online detection-plus-localization schemes \cite{li2021online}. The cited localization studies do not explicitly document sensitivity to network topology/meshedness or an ablation of relational features against a matched non-relational baseline, which is the comparison examined in Sections~\ref{sec:results1} and \ref{sec:scale}.

\subsection{Real-Time / Latency-Aware Classification}
\cite{abukhousa2026latency} provides the closest latency comparison: on a larger industrial electromagnetic-transient (EMT) testbed, classifier inference is fast while end-to-end latency remains substantially higher. Section~\ref{sec:latency} examines the same pipeline-level question and isolates a software-level bottleneck in the present implementation.

\subsection{Zero-Day / Unseen-Attack Generalization}
The general (non-power-specific) intrusion-detection literature widely reports that supervised detectors trained on known attack families do not reliably generalize to unseen ones; a verified, quantitative comparison specific to the power-systems FDIA literature remains an open point for future work.

\subsection{Broader Gaps Identified in Reviews}
A 2025 scoping review of machine-learning applications in power-system protection \cite{oelhaf2025scoping} identifies two relevant gaps: limited reporting of computational latency and little cross-topology evaluation. Sections~\ref{sec:latency} and \ref{sec:scale} address these issues within the scope of the present test systems.

\section{Testbed and Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Network}
A 5-bus, 0.4\,kV synthetic microgrid built in \texttt{pandapower}: point of common coupling, a PV bus modeled as a steady-state grid-following (GFL) power injection, a BESS bus modeled as a steady-state grid-forming (GFM) power injection, and two load buses, connected by 4 lines. Both are static \texttt{sgen} operating-point injections, not dynamic converter-control models -- no droop control or virtual inertia is simulated; the GFL/GFM labels describe each device's intended role in the microgrid, not modeled control dynamics. \texttt{net.f\_hz = 50.0} (verified from the model object, not assumed -- this fixes the one-cycle protection-relevant computational target below to 20\,ms).

\subsection{State Estimation}
State estimation uses weighted least squares (WLS) via \texttt{pandapower.estimation.estimate}, with 23 measurements per cycle: voltage magnitude and P/Q injection at all 5 buses, plus P/Q from-side flow on all 4 lines. This is a conventional model-driven state-estimation setting of the type reviewed in \cite{lin2026state}. Across all three test systems, residual features are the deployable normalized measurement residual $(z-h(\hat{x}))/\sigma$ computed from the WLS solution; no feature is constructed from an unobservable ground-truth state.

\subsection{Threat Model}
Two synthetic attack families, both targeting a randomly chosen load bus per scenario:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Naive}: a single voltage-magnitude sensor is spoofed by a fixed-range random offset.
\item \emph{Model-consistent (``stealth'') FDIA}: following the Liu--Ning--Reiter construction adapted to the AC case, the true state is perturbed at the target bus and the corresponding measurement-space attack vector $a = h(x_{\text{attack}}) - h(x_{\text{true}})$ is computed exactly, so the attack is, by construction, invisible to the chi-square residual test.
\end{itemize}
A benign hard-negative physical load disturbance (a 25--70\% load step at a randomly chosen bus) is included specifically to be confusable with an attack in raw residual terms.

\subsection{Prior Construction}
Every replication starts from one randomly sampled base operating point (a load/PV level, shared unmodified across that replication's 4 scenarios). Before any attack or physical disturbance is applied to build the ``true'' network for a given scenario, an independent copy of that same base operating point has its load values perturbed by a fresh, independently drawn forecast error ($\mathcal{N}(0, 2.5\%)$ per load at 5-bus, clipped to $\pm6\%$; $\mathcal{N}(0, 5\%)$ at IEEE 14-bus/30-bus, a cross-network noise-model difference documented as a limitation below) and is solved through its own, separate AC power flow -- this perturbed-and-solved copy is the ``prior.'' \texttt{prior\_only} features are the deviation between the WLS state estimate (computed from the scenario's actual, possibly-attacked measurements) and this prior's own voltage/angle solution. Critically, the prior is constructed \emph{before}, and entirely independently of, whichever scenario (clean, naive attack, stealth attack, physical disturbance) that replication's true network becomes -- it never sees the true or attacked state, only an independently-perturbed forecast of the same nominal base point. This is what lets stealth attacks, invisible in residual space by construction, become visible at all: the attack is only tuned to be consistent with the WLS residual test, not with an estimate the defender never let it see.

\subsection{Feature Sets}
Four feature sets are compared. \texttt{residual\_only}: conventional WLS measurement residuals. \texttt{prior\_only}: the prior-deviation features defined above. \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior}: the two combined, with no relational or structural information at all -- the topology-free ablation. \texttt{topology\_fusion}: \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} plus explicit relational features describing each bus relative to its network neighbors (local-minus-neighbor innovation/residual, incident-edge aggregates); no static structural metadata (node degree, device role) beyond that, since neither is a scenario-specific neighbor comparison and both risk leaking attack-target eligibility (the attack generator above only ever targets load buses). To preserve the intended ablation, non-relational baselines exclude all relational columns, and static node-degree/device-role metadata is excluded from every feature set because it could encode attack-target eligibility when attacks are restricted to load buses.

\subsection{Models and Evaluation}
LogisticRegression, RandomForest, and HistGradientBoosting classifiers are compared with replication-group-safe 70/15/15 train/validation/test splits and group-aware bootstrap confidence intervals. Model selection uses the validation split exclusively: each candidate model is scored on validation, the best-scoring model per feature set is selected, and only that model's (until then unseen) test-set score is reported.

\subsection{Sample-Size Terminology}
A \emph{replication} is one independently sampled base operating point (load level and forecast draw). Each replication produces four matched \emph{scenarios}: clean, naive attack, stealth attack, and physical disturbance. Thus, $N$ replications yield $4N$ scenarios. The 70/15/15 split is applied at the replication level so that matched scenarios from the same operating point cannot cross train, validation, and test partitions. With $N{=}500$, this produces approximately 300 test scenarios for detection and 150 cyber-only test scenarios for localization. Unless otherwise specified, $n$ denotes the resulting test-scenario count rather than the number of replications.

\subsection{Multi-Seed Replication}
Figure~\ref{fig:multiseed} summarizes the 16-seed result. With localization training restricted to cyber-scenario nodes only (matching the convention used at IEEE 14-bus/30-bus, Section~\ref{sec:scale}), the \texttt{topology\_fusion}-vs-\texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} detection gap is $-0.000\pm0.009$ (range $-0.013$ to $+0.017$, 95\% CI $[-0.005,+0.004]$), and the localization gap is $+0.003\pm0.017$ (range $-0.020$ to $+0.027$, 95\% CI $[-0.006,+0.012]$). Both confidence intervals cross zero, supporting a near-zero effect on this testbed for both tasks.

\subsection{Redundancy Diagnostic}
The near-zero 5-bus gaps are consistent with high linear recoverability of the relational features from the local feature set. Predicting each of the 65 relational columns from \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} alone, with 5-fold cross-validated ridge regression, gives a mean out-of-fold $R^2$ of 0.956 (median 1.000, minimum 0.613, 90.8\% of columns above 0.8): a neighbor-mean or incident-edge aggregate over a fixed, small neighbor set is, on a network this size, close to an affine function of the same per-bus values \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} already provides in full. A sufficiently flexible classifier (HistGradientBoosting, the best model throughout) may therefore reconstruct much of the relational information internally, consistent with the near-zero 5-bus gaps. This explanation is specific to the fixed 5-bus topology and does not predict the larger-network results in Section~\ref{sec:scale}, where a separate size-agnostic feature design is used. The Discussion evaluates whether reduced redundancy can explain the observed cross-network pattern.

\section{Results II: Real-Time Feasibility -- Diagnosing and Fixing the Gap}
\label{sec:latency}

Following \cite{abukhousa2026latency}, we measure the full per-cycle decision pipeline (state estimation $\rightarrow$ feature computation $\rightarrow$ classification) against a one-cycle reference of 20\,ms at the network's confirmed 50\,Hz. The 20\,ms value is used as a per-cycle computational reference rather than a universal protection requirement. One-time network and model setup is excluded.

\subsection{Initial Measurement}
Median 42.98\,ms, $2.15\times$ over budget, using the original pipeline (RandomForest classifier, \texttt{tolerance=1e-7}, \texttt{maximum\_iterations=40}, flat state-estimator initialization every cycle).

\subsection{Warm-Start Test}
Warm-starting the state estimator from the previous cycle's converged solution produced only a $1.06\times$ speedup (37.14\,ms), ruling out ``far from the solution'' as the dominant cost.

\subsection{Profiling}
Splitting the state-estimation call into its constituent steps showed the measurement-submission step cost 19.9\,ms -- essentially the same as the estimator's own solve (19.4\,ms). An ablation on \texttt{maximum\_iterations} (40 $\rightarrow$ 5: no speed change; 40 $\rightarrow$ 3: convergence failure in 19/20 trials) showed the true iteration requirement is roughly 4--5 steps regardless of the 40-iteration cap, ruling out iteration count as the estimator-side cost driver.

\subsection{Measurement-Update Optimization}
The measurement table was being rebuilt from scratch every cycle via a general-purpose element-creation API, called once per channel in a Python loop -- even though the table's structure is identical every cycle; only the values change. Replacing this with a one-time table construction followed by an in-place overwrite of the value column each cycle gave a $1158\times$ speedup on this sub-step (19.4\,ms $\rightarrow$ 0.017\,ms), verified to produce a bit-identical state-estimation result (max difference 0.0).

\subsection{Final Benchmark}
With a lighter classifier (LogisticRegression, competitive with RandomForest per Section~\ref{sec:results1}'s accuracy results and faster at inference), two independent $N{=}300$ reruns on the same documented environment, both with 0 convergence failures, give 16.155--17.015\,ms median, 16.429--18.211\,ms p95, 16.952--19.190\,ms p99, and 89.067--92.657\,ms max end-to-end latency (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} shows the most recent run). Median, p95, and p99 remain within the 20\,ms computational budget in both reruns, while the maximum exceeds it in both. Across the same two reruns, stage medians are 15.855--16.664\,ms for WLS state estimation, 0.050--0.057\,ms for post-estimation residual/innovation calculation, 0.083--0.088\,ms for feature computation, 0.0068--0.0076\,ms for array assembly, and 0.160--0.200\,ms for model inference; the WLS solve therefore dominates the budget. The benchmark environment is Apple arm64 running macOS 15.6.1 with Python 3.12.4, pandapower 2.14.10, NumPy 1.26.4, pandas 2.2.2, and scikit-learn 1.4.2.

The timing benchmark uses a separate 35-feature representation (7 local-plus-neighbor statistics per bus $\times$ 5 buses), rather than \texttt{topology\_fusion} or \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} verbatim. Feature computation is a small fraction of total latency, so this difference does not affect the bottleneck conclusion. The classifier is trained on 150 warm-up scenarios generated through the same simulation pipeline (83 attack, 67 clean; training balanced accuracy 1.000). The timer includes state estimation, residual/innovation calculation, feature computation, array assembly, and inference.

\subsection{Positioning}
\cite{abukhousa2026latency} reports sub-15\,ms classifier inference embedded in 50--90\,ms end-to-end pipelines on an industrial-grade EMT simulator and recommends hardware acceleration. In the present implementation, a large fraction of the latency gap instead comes from avoidable software overhead, indicating that pipeline profiling should precede hardware acceleration. A knowledge-distilled LightGBM detector for virtualized microgrids likewise reports only 54--67\,ms per 1000 samples ($\sim$0.06\,ms/sample) for inference \cite{ogiesoba2026cyberattack}, comparable in scale to the 0.16--0.20\,ms inference step here and far below the 15.9--16.7\,ms state-estimation step.

The detector achieves high recall on attack types represented in training but fails to extrapolate to a withheld attack mechanism, and this behavior is identical with and without topology-relational features: the two feature sets are indistinguishable on every cell of Table~\ref{tab:zeroday}, confirming Section~\ref{sec:results1}'s prediction that they are behaviorally interchangeable, here for a question -- generalization to an unseen mechanism -- that has nothing to do with topology specifically. Within the two-family attack taxonomy used here, no zero-day generalization is observed for either feature set. This complements, from the opposite direction, a related generalization gap documented in the fault-classification literature: an XGBoost-based classifier trained and evaluated on the \emph{same} distribution still drops from 96.8\% (synthetic data) to 85.2\% (physical data) \cite{senyuk2025bulk} -- i.e., even without an unseen attack \emph{type}, a synthetic-to-real distribution shift alone costs over 11 points. Together, these results motivate evaluating both attack-family shift and synthetic-to-real distribution shift before deployment. A deployment-aware zero-day evaluation in the adjacent EV/V2X charging-infrastructure domain \cite{sakr2026explainable} is a useful methodological reference for what a more rigorous version of Section~\ref{sec:zeroday}'s test could look like.

\section{Results IV: Cross-Network Evaluation}
\label{sec:scale}

Sections~\ref{sec:results1}--\ref{sec:zeroday} use the custom 5-bus microgrid. Cross-network evaluation is performed on IEEE 14-bus and IEEE 30-bus systems using a separate size-agnostic feature design.

\subsection{Test-System Selection}
IEEE 14-bus and IEEE 30-bus were selected because their \texttt{pandapower} bus indexing maps directly to the internal power-flow representation used by the evaluation pipeline (confirmed directly: \texttt{net.bus} count matches the internal power-flow-case bus count for both), and they are, independently, among the most commonly used benchmarks in the power-system state-estimation/FDIA literature specifically.

\subsection{Cross-Network Method}
All WLS/measurement/attack machinery is network-size-agnostic by construction and was reused unmodified. The original fixed per-bus feature columns do not generalize past 5 buses and were replaced with a size-agnostic design: global summary statistics of the residual/innovation arrays, plus, for localization, a trained per-node classifier ranking every bus's probability of being the attack target. This feature design is implemented separately from Section~\ref{sec:method}'s but applies the same safeguards: the residual feature is the deployable measurement residual $z - h(\hat{x})$ (never the simulator's hidden ground-truth state), model selection is validation-based rather than test-set-based, non-relational baselines exclude relational columns, and \texttt{topology\_fusion} excludes static structural metadata such as node degree, which could otherwise act as a target-eligibility prior because attacks are restricted to load buses. All numbers below use $N{=}500$ replications and 16 independent seeds at both networks.

\subsection{Cross-Network Result}
At 5-bus (Table~\ref{tab:gapstats}), both 16-seed gaps remain centered near zero with tight confidence intervals (Section~\ref{sec:results1}). At IEEE 14-bus, the detection gap is positive in 11 of 15 non-zero seeds (mean $+0.009\pm0.012$) and the localization gap is non-negative in all 16 seeds, zero seeds favoring \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} (mean $+0.018\pm0.017$). At IEEE 30-bus, the detection gap is positive in all 16 seeds (mean $+0.028\pm0.019$) and the localization gap is positive in 12 of 15 non-zero seeds (mean $+0.013\pm0.016$). Both tasks show positive mean gaps at both standard IEEE networks.

\subsection{Statistical Evidence at 16 Seeds}
\label{sec:scale-stats}
At 16 seeds (Table~\ref{tab:gapstats}), four of the six gaps have a $t$-based 95\% confidence interval excluding zero, with a two-sided $t$-test rejecting the null at $p<0.05$ for the same four (IEEE-14 detection $p=0.011$; IEEE-14 localization $p<0.001$; IEEE-30 detection $p<0.001$; IEEE-30 localization $p=0.007$). 

A distribution-free sign test, weaker but assumption-free, agrees for three of the four (IEEE-14 localization $p<0.001$; IEEE-30 detection $p<0.001$; IEEE-30 localization $p=0.018$) but not for IEEE-14 detection specifically ($p=0.059$, 11 of 15 non-zero seeds positive) -- evidence that a small effect relative to per-seed noise can clear a parametric test before it clears the sign test's coarser bar. 

Testing six gaps simultaneously raises a multiple-comparisons concern: a Bonferroni correction requiring $p<0.0083$ for family-wise 95\% confidence still clears IEEE-14 localization, IEEE-30 detection, and IEEE-30 localization, but not IEEE-14 detection. Three gaps (IEEE-14 localization, IEEE-30 detection, IEEE-30 localization) remain statistically significant at the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold. IEEE-14 detection is positive on average but is the weakest-supported IEEE effect, while both 5-bus gaps remain centered near zero ($p=0.849$ and $p=0.500$ by $t$-test). The 16-seed sample is pragmatic rather than based on a formal power analysis; additional seeds or a paired/bootstrap difference analysis would provide tighter, less assumption-dependent bounds on effect magnitude.

\subsection{Interpretation}
Across the three networks and two tasks, the 16-seed pattern is network-dependent under the tested settings rather than task-specific: both detection and localization show a small positive gap at both standard IEEE test systems, while the small custom 5-bus microgrid shows no such gap on either task. These results directly address the limited cross-topology evaluation identified in the 2025 scoping review by Oelhaf et al. \cite{oelhaf2025scoping}, while retaining matched test-set sizes and the same 16-seed standard across the three evaluated networks.

\section{Discussion}

Across Sections~\ref{sec:results1}--\ref{sec:scale}, topology-relational features show a network-dependent pattern under the tested settings (Section~\ref{sec:scale-stats}). At the small custom 5-bus microgrid, explicit topology-relational features show no advantage over a matched non-relational ablation on either detection (mean $-0.000\pm0.009$, 16 seeds) or localization (mean $+0.003\pm0.017$). At both standard IEEE test systems, the same ablation shows a small positive gap on both tasks: IEEE 14-bus detection ($+0.009\pm0.012$) and localization ($+0.018\pm0.017$); IEEE 30-bus detection ($+0.028\pm0.019$) and localization ($+0.013\pm0.016$). Both standard IEEE systems therefore differ qualitatively from the small custom microgrid on both tasks.

At 5-bus, the larger improvement comes from combining residual and prior-innovation information rather than from adding topology-relational features (Section~\ref{sec:results1}). Section~\ref{sec:results1}'s redundancy analysis gives the 5-bus null result a structural, not just empirical, footing: on a fixed, small topology, a relational aggregate is close to a linear function of information a flexible classifier already has (mean out-of-fold $R^2 = 0.956$), so there is little left for an explicit relational feature to contribute. If this redundancy argument explained the cross-network pattern, it should weaken at the larger, more meshed IEEE networks -- but the same diagnostic, applied to IEEE-14's relational localization features, gives a mean $R^2$ of 0.966 (median 0.999, 92\% of columns above 0.8), if anything \emph{higher} than 5-bus, not lower. The redundancy hypothesis alone therefore does not explain the cross-network pattern: relational information remains highly linearly recoverable from local features at IEEE-14, yet the explicit relational representation still yields a positive 16-seed localization gap. Linear recoverability does not imply that a fixed-depth tree ensemble will reconstruct the same aggregation reliably from a flat input. The diagnostic therefore rules out missing linear information as the explanation but does not identify the mechanism. An equivalent IEEE-30 redundancy diagnostic is not reported because its detection features use a different, non-comparable representation; developing a separate diagnostic for that representation is left for future work.

One possible explanation is feature discoverability: HistGradientBoosting receives a flat input and must infer useful neighbor combinations implicitly, whereas the topology-aware representation supplies those aggregations directly. This interpretation is consistent with the larger-network results but is not tested causally here.

Overall, the 16-seed results show small positive gaps at both standard IEEE systems and near-zero gaps at 5-bus. Because the test systems also differ in measurement/prior settings and other design details, the pattern is best interpreted as setup-dependent rather than as evidence that network identity alone causes the difference.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Scale and setup dependence}: Three network topologies are evaluated with 16 independent seeds each (Section~\ref{sec:scale-stats}). Both tasks show small positive gaps at the standard IEEE systems (14-bus, 30-bus), whereas both 5-bus gaps remain near zero. None uses a real (non-synthetic) network or load profile, and with only three networks tested, one custom and two standard, the finding that the two standard networks behave alike and differently from the custom one is consistent with, but does not prove, a genuine standard-vs-custom or scale/meshedness effect -- a fourth network, ideally another custom, non-standard design, would be needed to separate ``standard IEEE benchmark'' from ``larger or more meshed'' as the operative variable.
\item \textbf{Multiple comparisons and one borderline result}: four of the six gaps in Table~\ref{tab:gapstats} have a 95\% confidence interval excluding zero and $p<0.05$ by $t$-test; a Bonferroni correction for six simultaneous tests (requiring $p<0.0083$ for family-wise 95\% confidence) leaves three of four surviving -- IEEE-14 detection ($p=0.011$ uncorrected) is the weakest-supported of the four and does not clear the sign test's conventional threshold either ($p=0.059$). Even where statistically significant, effect sizes remain small (0.009--0.028 balanced-accuracy/top-1 points) relative to the much larger gain from combining residual and prior information over either alone (Section~\ref{sec:results1}). Sixteen seeds is itself a convenience choice, not a formal power calculation; a pre-registered sample size or a paired/bootstrap difference test, neither run here, would put a tighter, less assumption-dependent bound on the effects' true magnitude.
\item \textbf{Cross-network measurement-noise and forecast-uncertainty mismatch}: measurement standard deviations are fixed absolute values shared, unscaled, across all three networks despite their very different physical scales, and prior load-forecast uncertainty is 2.5\% at 5-bus vs.\ 5\% at IEEE-14/IEEE-30 (Section~\ref{sec:method}). This was not standardized or swept as an independent robustness axis; the cross-network comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:scale} should be read with this confound explicitly in mind, not as a controlled scale-only experiment. Accordingly, the cross-network pattern should be interpreted as \emph{setup-dependent under the tested measurement/prior regimes}: each result reflects topology together with its noise and prior configuration, not topology in isolation. IEEE-14 and IEEE-30 share an identical noise/prior convention, so comparisons between those two systems are not subject to this particular mismatch; comparisons that include 5-bus are.
\item \textbf{No zero-day generalization} (Section~\ref{sec:zeroday}): detection/localization numbers hold only for attack types represented in training -- verified for both \texttt{topology\_fusion} and \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior}, which collapse identically (Table~\ref{tab:zeroday}), under a corrected, symmetric evaluation protocol; localization was not separately re-tested for zero-day generalization (only detection was), and the underlying attack taxonomy is still limited to two families (item 5 below).
\item \textbf{Single testbed family, two attack types}: the present AC-consistent attack generator does not independently vary the compromised-measurement subset; introducing explicit attacker-access constraints (e.g.\ a fixed budget of compromisable sensors) as a separate, tunable threat-model parameter is left for future work.
\item \textbf{Latency results} (Section~\ref{sec:latency}) characterize one documented Apple-arm64/macOS 15.6.1 Python/\texttt{pandapower} environment, not a hardware or RTOS claim, and were not re-benchmarked on the smaller \texttt{residual\_plus\_prior} feature set (Section~\ref{sec:latency} argues this would not change the conclusion, since state estimation, not classification, dominates the budget, but this is an argument, not a re-measurement).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

Across a small custom inverter-dominated microgrid and two standard IEEE test systems (14-bus and 30-bus), explicit topology-relational features show a network-dependent pattern under the tested settings. At 16 seeds, both detection and localization show small positive gaps at both standard IEEE systems (IEEE-14: $+0.009\pm0.012$ detection, $+0.018\pm0.017$ localization; IEEE-30: $+0.028\pm0.019$ detection, $+0.013\pm0.016$ localization), while the small custom 5-bus microgrid shows no such gap on either task. The mechanism behind the difference between the standard IEEE systems and the custom microgrid remains unresolved (Section~\ref{sec:scale}), so the result should not be generalized beyond the tested settings. Separately, pipeline optimization reduces median latency from 42.98\,ms to 16.2--17.0\,ms, with p99 below the 20\,ms reference in both repeated runs but rare maxima above it; the detector also fails to generalize to attack types withheld from training.
